# Commentary I: The Ontological Collapse of the *Daimon* into *Êthos* (Heraclitus, DK 22 B119)

## Primary Text
> **ἦθος ἀνθρώπῳ δαίμων.**  
> *(Heraclitus, Fragment B119 Diels-Kranz / Stobaeus, Anthologium 4.40.23)*

## 1. Philological & Syntactic Analysis
The aphorism consists of three words without a copula (*estin*), creating a deliberate structural symmetry between *êthos* (character, habitual dwelling) and *daimon* (divine apportionment, external destiny). 

* **Shift in Word Order:** By placing *êthos* in the emphatic initial position and *daimon* in the predicate position, Heraclitus inverts the archaic Homeric hierarchy. In Homer (*Iliad* 1.222, *Odyssey* 3.27), the *daimon* acts upon the human being from without. Here, the human interior disposition becomes the subject that defines the divine apportionment.
* **The Dative of Reference (*anthrôpôi*):** Placed squarely between the two nouns, *anthrôpôi* ("for a human being") acts as the ontological hinge where external fate is internalized.

## 2. Interiority and the Emergence of the Self
Moving beyond purely ethical readings (which reduce *êthos* to moral habit), B119 marks a decisive moment in the Greek discovery of inwardness:
1. **Rejection of External Scapegoating:** Unlike Homeric heroes (e.g., Agamemnon blaming *Atê* and *daimon* in *Iliad* 19.86–88), the Heraclitean subject cannot externalize the source of action.
2. **The Depth of the Soul (*Psychê*):** Read alongside Fragment B45 (*"You would not find out the boundaries of soul, even by traveling along every path: so deep a logos does it have"*), B119 establishes that the divine agency is no longer located on Olympus, but within the unfathomable depth of human interiority.

## 3. Trajectory Toward Plato
This Heraclitean internalization prepares the ground for Plato’s *Timaeus* (90a), where the rational soul (*nous*) is explicitly identified as the personal *daimon* dwelling at the summit of the human being.
